One in Ten Parcels Came Back Unpaid. The Data Said Exactly Which Ones.

I built a marketplace dataset with real problems baked in, then wrote the SQL to find out where the money actually leaks. Cash on delivery was the villain, but not in the way I expected.

Cash on delivery is how most of Pakistan shops online. It is also how a lot of money quietly disappears, because a parcel someone refuses at the door costs freight in both directions and earns nothing.

Everybody in e-commerce knows this. What nobody seems able to say is which orders to worry about. "Reduce RTO" is not a decision anyone can act on. So I built a marketplace to find out.

First, the honest part

The data here is synthetic. I generated 68,155 orders across two and a half years for a fictional Pakistani marketplace I called Kaarobar, along with 36,543 customers, 637 products, 137 sellers and 98,799 checkout sessions.

I did that deliberately, and not because real data was unavailable to me. Clean data teaches you nothing. I wanted a dataset that fights back, so I designed the defects in first: an order system that "migrated" on 1 January 2025 and left two date formats in one column, shipping fees stored as text like Rs. 150, one city spelled 59 different ways, duplicate customer accounts, and quality-assurance test orders sitting in production.

Then I had to clean all of it before I could answer a single question. That part is the job. The findings below are real findings about this dataset, produced by real analysis. They are not claims about the Pakistani market.

The mess, by the numbers

Before changing anything, I profiled the raw data. That scorecard drove every cleaning rule that followed:

  • 18,203 orders still in the old DD/MM/YYYY format
  • 59 spellings of 16 cities, including KHI, lahore, Pindi and Abbotabad
  • 540 duplicate customer accounts under the same email
  • 272 exact duplicate order rows from an ingestion retry
  • 546 orders with no shipping city at all
  • 266 prices with an extra zero typed in
  • 45 orders placed by internal test accounts

Two date formats in one text column is the kind of thing that silently halves your dataset if you let the database decide. Parsing each row by its own shape fixes it:

CASE WHEN order_datetime LIKE '__/__/____ __:__'
     THEN TRY_CONVERT(datetime2(0), order_datetime, 103)
     ELSE TRY_CONVERT(datetime2(0), order_datetime, 120)
END AS ordered_at

TRY_CONVERT rather than CONVERT matters: a bad value returns NULL instead of killing the whole batch. Then a test asserts that zero timestamps failed to parse, so a silent loss becomes a loud failure.

The SQL Server quirk that nearly fooled me

Here is the one I did not see coming, and it is worth knowing if you work on SQL Server.

I counted the distinct spellings of city and got 35. The data actually contained 59. SQL Server compares text without regard to letter case and ignores trailing spaces, so lahore, Lahore and Lahore  all look identical to a plain COUNT(DISTINCT ...).

SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT shipping_city) AS what_you_see_by_default,
       COUNT(DISTINCT (shipping_city + '|') COLLATE Latin1_General_BIN2) AS actual_spellings
FROM raw.orders;

The binary collation forces a byte-for-byte comparison, and the appended marker character keeps a trailing space from being swallowed. Two numbers, same column: 35 and 59.

If I had trusted the default, my lookup table would have missed 24 spellings, thousands of orders would have landed in the wrong city, and every city-level number in this article would have been wrong in a way no error message would ever have told me about.

How it is built

Three layers, and the discipline is in keeping them apart.

raw holds the source files exactly as they arrived, every column as text so nothing is rejected on the way in. Nothing is ever edited here, which means I can always prove what the source actually said. clean is the corrected version, produced only by script, one written rule per defect. mart is what analysis reads: a fact table at one row per order, and a customer dimension at one row per customer.

Then eighteen tests run on every single build. Order IDs unique. Every timestamp parsed. Every label mapped. No delivery date earlier than its own order. No test accounts. Net revenue never negative. Sixteen must pass or the build stops. Two are warnings, because the condition they describe is genuine business messiness rather than a defect.

That is the part I would defend hardest in a job interview. Anyone can produce a chart. A number nobody tested is a number nobody should quote.

Finding 1: cash on delivery, but only in one corner

Cash is 61.5% of orders and 94% of refusals. That much is unsurprising. The useful part appears when you split by customer history and order value:

  • Cash, first-time customer, order above Rs 15,000: 24.34% refused
  • Cash, first-time customer, Rs 15,000 or less: 19.18%
  • Cash, repeat customer, above Rs 15,000: 16.65%
  • Cash, repeat customer, Rs 15,000 or less: 10.60%
  • Prepaid, any segment at all: 1.08% to 1.82%
IMAGE: images/04_rto_drivers.png
Caption: Refusal rate by payment type, customer history and order value. The first-order, high-value cash segment is the outlier.

One in four high-value first orders from a new cash customer never gets paid for. Meanwhile a repeat customer paying cash for a small basket refuses one time in ten, which is survivable.

That distinction is the whole point. A blanket cash-on-delivery policy would punish a large, mostly healthy segment to fix a small, badly behaved one. The intervention belongs on new customers spending big, and it belongs hardest in tier-3 cities, where cash refusal reaches 18.69% against 1.33% prepaid.

Across 30 months, refused parcels carried Rs 63.4M of goods and burned roughly Rs 2.7M in freight.

Finding 2: a bad first delivery costs you the customer

I grouped every customer by how their very first order ended, then measured whether they ordered again within 90 days.

  • First order delivered on time: 55.44% came back
  • First order cancelled: 50.21%
  • First order delivered late: 28.90%
  • First order refused at the door: 24.58%
IMAGE: images/07_first_order_retention.png
Caption: 90-day repeat rate by how the first order ended.

A late first delivery is associated with roughly half the repeat rate of an on-time one. Look at the cancelled row, though: customers forgive an order that never arrives more readily than one that arrives badly. Nothing is worse than a promise broken slowly.

This reframes logistics. Late delivery is not only an operations cost line, it is a customer acquisition cost you already paid and then threw away.

Finding 3: the checkout test worked, on mobile

The dataset includes an A/B test on a redesigned checkout, 98,799 sessions over two months. I removed 1,261 sessions shorter than three seconds as automated traffic, then checked the split had not broken: 48,595 against 48,463, chi-square p 0.67. Fine.

Overall lift was +4.54 percentage points, confidence interval 3.92 to 5.15. A clear win. Except it was not a win everywhere:

  • Android: +6.02 points (5.23 to 6.81)
  • iOS: +5.56 points (3.95 to 7.17)
  • Desktop: +0.16 points (−1.06 to 1.38)
IMAGE: images/11_ab_test.png
Caption: Conversion lift by device, with 95% confidence intervals. Desktop crosses zero.

The desktop interval crosses zero, which means the change did nothing there that we can distinguish from chance. Reporting the blended 4.54 and calling it a company-wide success would have been technically true and practically misleading.

Recommendation: ship on mobile, keep testing desktop.

Finding 4: five sellers, not the worst five

Return rates by seller are a trap. Rank them and you get a list of small sellers where four returns out of twelve items produced a scary-looking percentage that means nothing.

So I tested each seller against their own category's return rate with a one-sided binomial z-test, which accounts for how many items they actually shipped. Five sellers out of 137 are genuinely out of line, and the dominant return reason in that group is that the item did not match its description. That is a listing quality problem with a name and an owner, not a vague complaint about returns.

What I would tell the business

Ask for partial advance payment on high-value first orders from new cash customers. That is the 24% segment, it is small enough to police, and leaving the repeat cash customer alone avoids strangling a segment that performs acceptably.

Treat first-order delivery as a retention investment. Route first orders to the courier with the best on-time record for that city tier, even at higher cost, and judge it on the 90-day repeat rate rather than on delivery cost alone.

Ship the checkout redesign on mobile only, and run the desktop version as its own experiment.

And measure all of it as an experiment rather than a rollout. Refusal rate on its own is a gameable metric, since you can always improve it by shipping fewer orders. Read it alongside completed orders and net revenue per shipped order or you will optimise yourself into a smaller business.

What this does not prove

The retention findings are associations, not causes. Showing that faster first deliveries cause repeat orders needs an experiment: route a random subset differently and watch what happens. Courier prices are not in the dataset, so the courier comparison weighs service quality only. And there are no delivery attempt logs, so a customer who refused cannot be separated from a courier who never showed up.

Saying that out loud costs nothing. Pretending otherwise costs everything the first time someone checks.

The stack, briefly

T-SQL on SQL Server 2025 Express for the pipeline, driven from Python through sqlcmd. pandas, Matplotlib and Seaborn for the statistics and charts. The whole database rebuilds from the source files in about a minute, every figure in this article regenerates from scratch, and the outputs are byte-identical between runs. The database is disposable. The SQL is the asset.

Code, queries and charts are all on GitHub: github.com/Abdulsamee-As/kaarobar-marketplace-analytics

The data is synthetic and Kaarobar is not a real company. The methods, the SQL and the mistakes are real.
